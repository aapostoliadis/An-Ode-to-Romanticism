The show

Nine paintings by J. M. W. Turner play one after another in five chapters: Dawn, The sea, Tempest, Fire and speed, and Light and colour. Each painting arrives with its own transition, paints itself in (colour first, then the strokes), and stays on the wall for 38 seconds (Time on the wall in the editor changes this), its title fading in and out. Each painting opens fully zoomed out and fills the screen edge to edge (on a screen of a different shape a little of its top and bottom or its sides is left out; on a phone held upright the whole painting is in view), and the camera barely moves: it drifts in a little toward the focal point and back. The life is in the painting itself, its ships pitching on the swell, waves rolling in, clouds, mist and light moving. After the last painting the show starts again.

Move the pointer over the painting to stir the wet paint; press and drag to stir harder. During the show the controls fade away after a few seconds; move the pointer to bring them back. If your system asks for reduced motion, the show starts paused and every movement is gentler.

Paintings

The strip at the bottom holds every painting in the show. Click one to go to it; the bar along the bottom of the current tile shows how long it has left on the wall.

Your painting adds any image to the show. Choose a file, paste the address of an image, or drag an image file onto the window. The program measures its brushstrokes, finds the light source and the horizon, separates sky, water and land, and writes a starting animation for it (warm, bright images get fire). Files always work. Some websites do not share their images' pixels, so an address from them cannot be animated; download the image and add the file instead. Added paintings stay for the session, and their edits are kept in the browser.

The Turner reproductions come from Wikimedia Commons. If it cannot be reached, a painted study, labelled as such, stands in for the painting so the show still runs.

Moving elements

Moving elements are objects cut out of the painting and set in motion, like the train on the viaduct or a boat on the swell. The painting behind them is repainted, so nothing is left behind when they move. Up to three per painting.

Press Add moving element, drag its numbered marker onto the object and choose how it moves. The dashed outline is the area that is cut out.

Comes toward the viewer
Sets off from where it is painted and runs toward you in perspective, growing and gathering speed, then the next one appears in its place. Drag the ⊙ marker up the track or road behind it, to the point it comes from.
Drifts across
Glides slowly sideways over the length of the painting's time on the wall, bobbing a little.
Rocks on the waves
Pitches and bobs about its waterline, like a ship on the swell.
Rises or sets
Climbs or sinks slowly, like the sun.
Pulses
Swells and settles gently, like a breathing light.
Flies away
Leaves from where it is painted with beating wings, like the gull in the Slave Ship, grows smaller as it goes and fades; then the next one arrives in its place.
Motion
How much it moves; 0 holds it still.
Size
Fits the outline to the object.
Tilt
Turns the outline to lie along a diagonal object.
Foam
Fluffy white water pushed ahead of a boat's bow and along its hull; 0 for none.
Flags
Makes the red flags and pennants in the upper part of the cut flutter in the wind; 0 holds them still.
Remove
Puts the object back into the painting.

Compact objects that stand out from their surroundings cut best, such as a dark boat on bright water or a sun in haze. An object painted in the same colours as what surrounds it moves only partly. The train in Rain, Steam and Speed, the ships in Ulysses and the Slave Ship and the gull use outlines traced by hand, which is why they move whole. If your copy of a painting is framed a little differently and a cut looks off, drag the element's marker in the editor: its outline moves with it.

Sound

The music, Suspended by Light, starts with your first click or key press and loops through the whole show without a break: each time round, the end crossfades into the beginning. The speaker in the top bar turns it on or off. Under it each painting has its own sound effects: surf, gulls and creaking timber for Ulysses, the river and birdsong at Norham, the tug's engine and a ship's bell for the Temeraire, a fearsome sea with booming, crashing waves and cries for help on the Slave Ship, the roaring storm around the steam-boat, wind, thunder, soldiers' voices in no real language and the clink of metal in the Alps, the fire across the river with its alarm bells and people crying out, an old steam locomotive that whistles as it sets off and grows louder and quicker as it comes toward you, and in Light and Colour glassy tones, sad murmuring voices, sobbing and people wading through the water. The light of the painting pulses with the music.

The effects follow the sliders: more rain, snow or fire in the editor means more of it in the sound. In the editor's Sound group, Mood sets the character of the painting, Sound effects picks a different soundscape (or "No effects"), and Effects level sets how loud they are under the music. Choose your own music in the top bar plays your own track instead of Suspended by Light.

Saving

Record records the painting exactly as it plays, titles included and without the controls, as a WebM video (MP4 in Safari), with the music and effects when sound is on. Press Stop and the video downloads. The ◉ button saves the current frame as a PNG image. Edits to a painting are saved automatically in this browser; use Export and Import in the editor to move them to another computer.

How the paintings come alive

The Carrières des Lumières shows (Culturespaces, AMIEX process) project thousands of digitised artworks across 7,000 m² of quarry walls, pillars and floor, set in motion and cut to a soundtrack. This program rebuilds that vocabulary in real time on the GPU.

  1. Strokes that move. The direction of every brushstroke is measured from the image, and the paint is advected along it with a looping flow map, so skies and seas churn the way Turner painted them.
  2. Layers. Each painting is separated into sky, water and land, with figures cut out and the brush texture lifted off the colour. Clouds drift, water ripples, foliage stirs, light drifts and the paint swirls, each layer on its own.
  3. Depth. The layers follow the camera at slightly different speeds, sky least and land most, so the flat canvas opens up in depth.
  4. A still camera. Each painting opens fully zoomed out and the camera only drifts in a little and back, so the eye stays on the painting's own motion.
  5. Moving elements. Objects are cut out of the painting, the background behind them is repainted, and they move: the train sets off from where Turner painted it and runs down the viaduct toward you, boats pitch on the swell with white water at their bows and flags flying, suns rise and set.
  6. Living atmosphere. Mist drifts, smoke and steam rise, fire flickers, embers rise, rain slants and snow falls.
  7. Light. A breathing glow and rays from the light source, pulsing with the music, with shafts of light fanning out from the sun.
  8. Sound. Suspended by Light plays as the background music, with a soundscape for each painting under it.
  9. The painting paints itself. Colour washes first, then the strokes, spreading out from the light.
  10. Transitions. Brushstroke wipes, watercolour bleeds, floods of light, melting paint, mosaics.
